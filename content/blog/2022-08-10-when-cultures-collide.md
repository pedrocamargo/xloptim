<span style="font-weight: 400">I have now been a professional for at least 6 years on 3 different continents, which is terrible for my retirement
savings and professional network development but gives me an interesting perspective on different work cultures and their impact on people’s lives 
and (professional) relationships. As a practical guy, I always though a lot about the mechanics of these changes, but it turns out that culture, 
or the social contract, can be most difficult aspect of moving abroad. I guess it it worth describing the cultural collision I allude on the title
with a brief history of my moves so far.</span>


<span style="font-weight: 400">Back in 2008 when we decided to move from Brazil to California, I was a little bit worried about my language skills and whether I would 
be able to secure a position at a good PhD program, but I was never worried about cultural differences, be that around food, music (I do love Brazilian music) or
the general social contract. That was an oversight, but one that bore no consequences.</span>

<span style="font-weight: 400">My 5 years in Orange County, 4 of which doing my PhD, proved to be fairly easy on the cultural integration side, as the melting 
pot that is Southen California allows for anybody to blend in by sticking out. The environment at UC Irvine was always very collaborative, and cultural 
differences proved to be not an asset nor a hindrance. That time at UCI, however, reinforced my love for intellectual debate and coincided with my discovery 
of the great late Christopher Hitchens, the best debater I have ever seen. </span>

<span style="font-weight: 400">Fast-forward and we decided to spend more time in Australia than in the US and have the kids attend school in Brisbane (an absolutely 
lovely city). This time my language problems were going to be much smaller and cultural differences would mean a much smaller move than the one to the US. Or so I 
thought.</span>

<span style="font-weight: 400">Australia turned out to be quite different from the US, with certain aspects of daily lives and conveniences being a lot more similar 
to Brazil instead, and we found ourselves navigating a sea of differences as big as we had done when moving to the USA. Cultural differences were clear, but it took
us some time to identify patterns.</span>

<span style="font-weight: 400">It didn't take *too* long for me to notice that people seemed to be a bit uncomfortable any time I criticized a piece of work that they had
done or contributed to, although I was so consumed with moving the family and getting the kids sorted and downright entrenched into delivering projects that
had been months late when I started working here, that I didn't give it too much thought at first.</span>

<span style="font-weight: 400">As time passed and I started to interact more with people in other teams, junior staff and clients, it became painfully clear that
conducting business in Australia was significantly different than doing so in the USA or Brazil. I learned terms like "compliment sandwich", which describes the principle
by which every criticism needs to be preceded with and followed by a compliment, and I remember time-sheeting 30h *wordsmithing* (another term I learned here) communications
with a client to explain that the methodology they were implemented did not make sense without offending them (it was downright absurd).</span>

<span style="font-weight: 400">My hypothesis is that this aversion to giving and receiving criticism has same roots as the so-called 
"Tall Poppy Syndrome", and there are tangential arguments for such just across the pond in New Zealand, with *Te Ara* putting it as “New Zealanders have 
sometimes been resentful of achievement – as though excellence or superiority in a particular field somehow represented a rebuff to ideas of equality.”. I have not found
other people that agree with this hypothesis, however.</span>

<span style="font-weight: 400">This cultural trait seems to also get conflated with the recent tendency in Western culture where there is an over-correction towards 
worrying about people's feelings and opinions, in detriment of honest and respectul intelectual debate.</span>

<span style="font-weight: 400">And have you all noticed that before I made ANY criticism to ONE aspect of Australian culture, I praised the country and other aspects of its culture first? As it turns out, saying that Australians are not receptive to criticism is seen a criticism and not taken well.</span>
